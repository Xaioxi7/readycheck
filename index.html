Before you start

About ReadyCheck

Please read this before your first check. You will only see it once.

What it does

A short reaction test while the camera looks at your eyelids, to help you understand how alert you are before duty.

It is not a medical device

It does not diagnose anything and does not decide whether you are fit to fly. That decision stays with you and your airline's normal process.

Camera

The camera is used only during a check. Video is analysed on this phone and is never recorded, stored or sent.

Your results

Results are saved on this phone. Your employer only learns whether you met the check requirement each month (a check on every flight day and at least 12 check days in every 28). Never your results, scores, eye data or how you felt. Anything else is shared only if you choose, and you can delete everything in Settings.

Prototype

How it works

1. Reaction test (primary measure)

The standard 3-minute brief Psychomotor Vigilance Test (PVT-B; Basner, Mollicone and Dinges, 2011), as used on tablets in daily air medical operations (Mollicone et al., 2023): a millisecond counter appears every 1 to 4 seconds (including 1 second of feedback); responses under 100 ms or with no counter showing are false starts; responses of 355 ms or more are lapses. The test always runs the full 3 minutes. Results are grouped by lapses plus false starts: 6 or fewer, 7 to 16, more than 16 (Basner et al., 2022).

Touchscreens add delay. Following NASA's touchscreen PVT validation, 77 ms (the delay measured on an iPad) is subtracted from every touch response. Real phones differ, so a production version would calibrate each device. The 3 practice tries are not scored.

2. Eyelids (supporting measure)

MediaPipe Face Landmarker tracks eyelid landmarks during the reaction test. It records PERCLOS (share of time eyes are at least 80% closed) and long closures of 500 ms or more. In a 2025 study, PERCLOS was the most informative facial feature for predicting PVT performance (Subramaniyan et al., Sleep). Eye signs can raise concern by one level but never lower it.

That study filmed at 240 frames per second. Browsers usually give 30, so short blinks and eyelid speed cannot be measured here.

3. Your own baseline

After 5 checks, your median reaction time is compared with your own history. The PVT shows no practice effect, which makes personal baselines practical. Comparing with yourself also cancels out your phone's screen delay.

4. Feeling vs measured

People under ongoing sleep loss often stop noticing how impaired they are (Van Dongen et al., 2003). When your rating and the test disagree, ReadyCheck tells you. It never tells someone who feels exhausted that they are fine.

5. Today's advice

After every check you get a short message for today, based on your level (Ready, Caution or Low alertness) and whether you are flying. Sleep advice follows the adult recommendation of 7 or more hours (American Academy of Sleep Medicine and Sleep Research Society, 2015). On a flight day at Low alertness, ReadyCheck reminds you that under 14 CFR 117.5 a pilot who is too fatigued must not accept the duty. It never decides for you: that stays with you and your airline's normal process.

6. Long-term status

One word, Good, Watch or Not good, from your own checks over the last 1 to 2 weeks. Only one day counts at a time. A low day is a day with a check at Caution or lower or, once your baseline exists after 5 checks, a median reaction time at least 2 robust standard deviations slower than your own usual.

  • Not enough data: fewer than 3 check days in the last 7.
  • Watch: 3 or more low days in the last 7, or your 7-day typical reaction time rising (by 5 ms or more) for 3 checks in a row.
  • Not good: in the last 14 days, at least 6 check days with low days on more than half of them, or feeling moderately tired or worse (5 or higher on the 7-point Samn-Perelli scale) on more than half.
  • Back to Good: 3 normal check days in a row. One bad day followed by recovery never changes your status.

Why these windows: over the first days of short sleep, performance declines and then levels off at a worse level, and it is not fully restored after 3 nights of recovery sleep (Belenky et al., 2003, Journal of Sleep Research). Two weeks of 6-hour nights produced deficits equal to up to 2 nights without any sleep, while people stopped noticing (Van Dongen et al., 2003, Sleep). Two weeks is also the standard screening window for when to seek help: the PHQ-9 asks about the last 2 weeks (Kroenke et al., 2001).

These thresholds are design rules built on that evidence. They are not a diagnosis.

Your plan on History picks up to 4 suggestions from your last 7 days, such as more sleep, a short nap, a steady wake time, water on duty or daylight exercise. Each one shows the data behind it. Contacting a support partner is suggested only when your status is Not good or after a hard duty you report.

7. Monthly check requirement

Your employer learns only one thing: whether you met the requirement this month. It is one check on every flight day and at least 12 check days in every 28 days. Only one check per calendar day counts.

  • A valid weekly picture needs at least 3 valid data points per week (Plews et al., 2014, International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance). 12 in 28 days is 3 a week.
  • 28 days matches the FAA's 672-consecutive-hour cumulative window (14 CFR 117.23).
  • Every flight day matches the fit-for-duty statement each pilot makes before each flight (14 CFR 117.5(d)).

The 12-in-28 number is a design rule derived from these sources. It is not a regulation. In this prototype, flight days are the days you answer "On duty" to "What's on today?". A real version would use the airline roster.

Limitations

  • Not validated. The thresholds come from laboratory studies, mostly of total sleep deprivation.
  • Moderate fatigue, such as one poor night, is harder to detect.
  • PVT-BA was validated after the fact, not in real time.
  • The fatigue forecast is illustrative only. A real product would use a validated model such as SAFTE-FAST.
  • Long-term status and the monthly requirement are design rules, not validated clinical thresholds.
  • This is a reference for the pilot, not a fit-to-fly decision.
